The Eucharist

Participation in the Eucharist
Does Not Confer Forgiveness of Sins

A verse-by-verse examination of whether the Eucharist confers forgiveness of sins — including an honest look at the passages used to argue the other side.

Why am I writing this?

I’ve heard many different views on the Eucharist, specifically in its necessity to be saved, to remain saved, or to receive forgiveness of sins. As I’ve covered in other writings, my position is that the Bible is clear that only purely believing/trusting in Jesus to the exclusion of any other physical works. Participation in what are called “means of grace”, as the Eucharist is labeled, is thus excluded, otherwise faith/belief/trust would not be sufficient to save, contradicting numerous Scriptures.

What Am I Not Saying?

I am NOT coming to a conclusion on whether the Eucharist is purely symbolic or not; I see the verses used for either position and I have not come to a complete conclusion myself that accounts for every verse. I aim to tackle this later, but I need much more time and study to do so.

  • Opposition Claim: Eating the bread and cup is a real koinōnia — participation — in the body and blood of Christ, set in parallel with Israel becoming "participants in the altar" by eating the sacrifices and with pagans communing with demons at their feasts. In each case eating joins the eater to the spiritual reality and its effects; that is the whole basis of Paul's warning. If eating idol-meat really binds one to demons, eating the Lord's bread and cup really binds one to Christ and confers the benefits of union with him — life and the forgiveness flowing from his sacrifice.

Paul’s breakdown of the Eucharist is in the next chapter. This is why the passage in question starts with “Therefore, my beloved, flee from idolatry” and ends with “Shall we provoke the Lord to jealousy? Are we stronger than he?”
  • Now let’s tackle the claim of “eating idol-meat really binds one to demons”. This struggles to stand in light of the rest of chapter 10 and chapter 8.
    • Continuing in chapter 10, Paul tells us in verse 25 to “Eat whatever is sold in the meat market without raising any question on the ground of conscience.” If we are told that such meat was offered in sacrifice, then we are to not eat it for the sake of the unbeliever’s conscience, with ours unaffected. Paul caps this off by saying “If I partake with thankfulness, why am I denounced because of that for which I give thanks?” The Greek word for partake here is the same as used in verse 21: “You cannot partake of the table of the Lord and the table of demons.”
    • In chapter 8, Paul tells us that we actually have the right to eat food sacrificed to idols, because we know that “’an idol has no real existence,’ and that ‘there is no God but one.’” Paul doubles down that whether the food is offered to God or demons, “Food will not commend us to God. This is the second reason why viewing chapter 10 as a literal participation by eating bread/food is problematic.

  • Opposition Claim: The cup is "the new covenant in my blood," and the bread "my body, which is for you," handed over to be eaten and drunk repeatedly until he comes. Proclaiming "the Lord's death" is not recalling an absent event but applying its benefit at the table; the death whose benefit is forgiveness is made present to the recipients each time they partake. The command "do this" makes the Supper the standing, efficacious means of receiving what Christ's death accomplished.

The word for proclaim, as shown above, in no way implies ‘applying benefits’ as the opposition claim states; it implies proclamation/declaration, in context about “the Lord’s death until He comes”.

  • “The command ‘do this’ makes the Supper the standing, efficacious means of receiving what Christ's death accomplished.” This claim is not supported by two fronts, the rest of that sentence, and the conclusion of the passage.
    • The rest of the sentence is “Do this in remembrance of me” and “Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me.” It does not say ‘do this to have my sacrifice applied to you’ or anything like that.
    • The conclusion of the passage is “For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes.” The word “for”, as the Greek shows above, directly links what was just said (the “Do this in remembrance of me” statements). So what does Paul say we should conclude? Not that the Lord’s Supper is a ‘standing, efficacious means of receiving what Christ’s death accomplished’, but that “as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes.”
  • Paul’s last verse in this passage is the most telling in terms of how we should interpret the purpose of the Lord’s Supper. Paul does not communicate that participation in the bread and the cup is what confers/applies forgiveness, but that as often as we do this, we proclaim/declare the Lord’s death until He comes.

1 Corinthians 11:27–32

  • Opposition Claim: The strongest argument that the Supper has real spiritual effect: eating unworthily makes one "guilty concerning the body and blood of the Lord" and brings real consequences — weakness, illness, even death — as a divine judgment tied to the eating itself. If unworthy eating produces real spiritual harm, then worthy eating produces real spiritual good. A meal that can damn cannot be inert; the same objective reality that brings judgment on the unworthy brings life and forgiveness to the worthy.
  • The Greek word for therefore is ὥστε (hōste - Strong’s 5620), translating to “so that”, “therefore”, “so as to”.
    From Helps Word-studies: hṓste (a conjunction, derived from 5613 /hōs, "as" and 5037 /té, "both-and") – wherefore (with the result that both . . . ), connecting cause to necessary effect which emphasizes the result (the combined, end-accomplishment). The result involved then is the combination of both elements in the correlation, underscoring the inevitable effect of the paired elements.
    • Shows a connection between what was just said (”For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes.”) to what is about to be said (this passage).

Verse 32 reads: “But when we are judged by the Lord, we are disciplined so that we may not be condemned along with the world.” The judgement that Paul describes here is not judgement in the condemnation sense, but judgement in the disciplinary sense.     Paul does not conclude by saying ‘when you come together to eat, ensure that each of you acknowledge that the elements are truly Christ’s body, lest you incur condemnation.’ Rather, Paul lands his argument where this topic began, a rebuke to division and inequality between members of Christ’s body.
  • If you insist in the condemnation type of judgment interpretation, then there’s an important question to ask: ‘why does someone who eat and drinks unworthily/without discerning the body eat and drink judgement on theirselves?’ Is it because the Eucharistic elements contain forgiveness when thought of a specific way, or is it because the Eucharist is a proclamation of the Gospel that when rejected leaves condemnation? Given the context that the Lord’s Supper proclaims the Lord’s death until He comes, I’d argue the latter. If someone partakes in this form of the Gospel presentation, but does not accept/trust Jesus’ sacrifice, then their fate is the same as those who reject the Gospel: condemnation/guilt for their sin.
